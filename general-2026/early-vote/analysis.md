*Compiled Oct. 28–30, 2026, from early-vote data through Wednesday, Oct. 28 (Day 10). Calls are the analyst's judgment from public data, not a model. The Friday/Saturday/Sunday decision thresholds are in [`benchmarks.md`](benchmarks.md).*

- **The lead is shrinking:** Republican-primary voters outvoted Democratic ones by about 40,000 on Days 9–10, as expected.

## Who's voting (Ryan, Day 8, change in share vs. 2022 same day)

- Ages 18–29: +4.2 pts. Ages 65+: −4.3 pts. (Through Day 10, turnout is still 48% among 65+ vs. 19–21% among under-35s.)
- Hispanic: +3.0 pts, to 18.4% of early voters.
- Women outnumber men by 434,872, vs. 172,170 at this point in 2022.
- 20% of early voters (about 1M) voted in neither the 2018 nor the 2022 midterm.

## Historical lean of non-primary voters (implied)

Derived from Ryan's final 2018/2022 totals plus actual results, assuming 95% of primary voters back their party.

| Year | Implied non-primary vote |
|---|---|
| 2018 (Cruz +2.6) | ~61–39 D |
| 2018 adjusted for today's classification* | ~D +10 to +14 |
| 2022 (Abbott +10.9) | ~52–48 R |

\*Before 2018, Democratic primaries were small (Ryan's 2018 file counted only 1.69M Democratic-primary voters, while Beto got 4.05M votes), so many Democrats sat in the "general-only" group. Record Democratic primaries in 2018, 2020 and 2026 moved many into the Democratic column; about 3.3M registered voters now have Democratic primary history.

**Working assumption for 2026:** non-primary voters lean **D +5 to +12**. Evidence: Hispanic polling D +10 to +18 (UH Hobby, Univision, Emerson); a younger, more female electorate; Ryan's note that these voters lean "center and/or left." Counter-evidence: 2022's R +4 and 2024's new-voter surge, which helped Trump. Sensitivity: each 5 points of lean moves the statewide result about 2 points.

**Confidence revision (Oct. 30):** the ~55/45 figure was an extra hedge that treated a 2022-style break among non-primary voters as roughly even odds. Evidence reviewed since then shows the 2024 shift among occasional voters has largely reversed:
- 2025 NJ and VA results
- State legislative specials averaging D +5.6 (Ballotpedia)
- 27 Democratic flips to Republicans' 0 (NBC)
- Texas Hispanic voters swinging ~11 pts toward Democrats (Univision)
- In a non-presidential year, Trump's occasional voters tend to stay home

That makes a 2022-style break a 10–15% tail. The central estimate and the D +5 to +12 lean are unchanged; only the uncertainty narrows, from ±2.7 to ±2.3 pts. The turnout-only 75% thresholds are in [`benchmarks.md`](benchmarks.md).

**Polling context:**
- Senate: polls are roughly tied.
- Governor: average Abbott +2.5 (PollCheck: 48.8–46.3); range Hinojosa +1 (YouGov) to Abbott +4 (Marist).
- In 2018, final polls had Cruz +6 to +9, and he won by 2.6. Polls tend to miss toward the side driving a turnout surge, but in 2020 and 2024 they underestimated Republicans in Texas.

## Down-ballot statewide races

### Method: baseline plus candidate adjustments

1. **Generic baseline.** How a typical Democrat vs. a typical Republican would do, given the turnout. Our central estimate is Talarico +2. Paxton runs slightly behind a typical Republican, so the baseline is about **D +1.5**. The baseline moves with the scenarios: baseline = Talarico margin − 0.5.
2. **Candidate adjustment.** Each race's polls compared with the average across races, halved where undecideds are high or the Democrat is little known. Late money and low name recognition pull those voters back toward their usual party.
3. **Down-ballot drop-off.** Texas ended straight-ticket voting in 2020, so each race must be marked separately. New and occasional voters, who lean Democratic this year, skip lower races more often. The drop-off is an estimate, not a measurement.

| Candidate | Evidence | Adjustment (+ = toward Republican) | Drop-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Abbott (Gov.) | Poll average Abbott +2.5; incumbent; money | +2 | 0 |
| Paxton (Senate) | Polls roughly tied; scandals | −0.5 | 0 |
| Patrick (Lt. Gov.) | Goodwin narrowly ahead in Aug. (A&M/Bush School, 48–46) and UT (late Sept.–early Oct.). But Patrick has ~$24M cash vs. Goodwin's ~$643K, has spent ~$14.5M on ads vs. ~$26K, and 56% didn't know Goodwin in July | −1 (polls imply −2.5; halved) | 0.75 |
| Middleton (AG) | Middleton 39–38 in July (Texas Public Opinion Research, Democrat-run); 41–36 in June (Texas Politics Project); ~18% undecided | +1 | 0.75 |
| Sheets (Ag.) | No general-election polls. Beat incumbent Sid Miller 52.7–47.3 in the primary. Tucker has filed an eligibility challenge against him | 0 | 1 |

**Democratic margin = baseline − adjustment − drop-off**

### Takeaways

- In the central case, all three down-ballot races are toss-ups.
- Every race moves point-for-point with the baseline, so the Saturday benchmarks and election-night signals apply to all of them.
- A Talarico +4 night likely means a Democratic sweep of the statewide offices; a Talarico +1 night means Republicans keep most of them.
- **Weakest assumptions:** the halved candidate adjustments and the drop-off size. Down-ballot polling is thin and months old.
- **Related:** Middleton's AG run leaves SD-11 open, one of the Republican Senate seats some forecasters rate only "Lean R."

## Tarrant County (thru Oct. 28)

- 482,044 votes, 35.9% turnout. That's already about 82% of Tarrant's entire 2022 vote (~590,000).
- Turnout vs. 2022 same day: +11.7 pts at Day 8, the largest gain of any big county.
- Democratic-primary voters 175,107; Republican-primary voters 147,858: **D +27,249**. For scale, Abbott carried Tarrant by ~24,000 in 2022.
- The lead held through Days 9–10 while the statewide lead shrank.
- Republicans have almost no reserve here: ~80,500 Republican-primary voters not yet voted vs. ~74,500 Democratic.
- Past results: Beto +4,308 (2018); Biden +~1,800 (2020); Abbott +~24,000 (2022); Trump +~42,000 and Allred +~1,800 (2024).

## District maps (Transparency USA, thru Oct. 28)

The maps shade by which party's past primary voters lead among early voters. **They are not vote counts.**
- **In South Texas**, Democratic-primary history overstates Democratic strength. Local races are decided in Democratic primaries, so conservative Hispanic voters vote in them. Several "blue" seats there are held by Republicans: HD-31, HD-37, HD-80, HD-34.
- **Purple (close) in Republican suburbs** is the stronger warning sign for Republicans.

**What the maps showed** (district numbers are read from the maps and not yet confirmed by tapping each district):
- **Congress:** purple in TX-9 (east Houston) and a seat east of Dallas, likely TX-32. Light blue in TX-23, TX-15, TX-28, TX-34 and TX-35.
- **State Senate:** purple in SD-9 and other seats around DFW.
- **State House:**
  - Western and southern Tarrant has the most purple of any metro (likely HD-93, 94, 96, 97, 99).
  - The edges of Harris County are purple.
  - North San Antonio is purple (likely HD-121 or 122).
  - Williamson County is pink, with no flip signal in the Austin suburbs.
  - Roughly 10–12 Republican-held House seats are in play; Democrats need +14.

## Corrections made during the analysis

- An early claim that "Republican-primary voters usually lead the early vote" was stated before it was checked. Ryan's Day 8 report later confirmed it (2022: R +436,528; 2024: R +794,322).
- An earlier claim that the 2026 share of non-primary voters was "about even with 2020/2024" compared Day 8 with a Day 3 figure. On the same day, 2024 was 41% vs. 30% now. Corrected: above the last midterm, below the last presidential year.
- "Hispanic voters lean Republican" was flipped to a pro-Democratic signal after reviewing 2026 polling.
- The first governor call (Abbott +3 to 6) was inconsistent with the Senate call and the polls. Revised to Abbott 0–3.
- An early benchmark table overstated how much the early-vote margin moves the result. Revised: about 1 point per 100,000 votes of margin.
